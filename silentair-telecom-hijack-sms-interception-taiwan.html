SilentAir spies hijack Asian telecom gear to intercept calls and 2FA texts

Researchers say a state-level group compromised equipment inside mobile operators to divert calls and texts, mostly targeting Taiwanese officials and VIPs.

By The Threat DeskNovember 13, 2026

Telecom security firm Enea has exposed a group it calls SilentAir that broke into equipment inside Asian mobile operators and used it to intercept phone calls and text messages. Enea assesses with high confidence that it is a state-level actor, but links the activity to China only with low confidence.

How it worked

The attackers used the hacked equipment to send signaling messages across the network that connects mobile carriers worldwide. Those messages falsely claimed the compromised equipment was now serving a target's phone, so the target's calls and texts were rerouted to the attackers. Enea also saw large numbers of silent SMS messages, which appear to have been used to check whether the diversions were still working.

For some targets, two-factor authentication codes were intercepted right after a diversion, suggesting attempts to break into other accounts. Enea did not say whether any account was taken over.

Who was targeted

Most targets were in Taiwan, including political and government figures. Others were in Southeast and East Asia, with occasional targets in East Africa, Central Asia, the Middle East and Europe. Activity came in month-long bursts separated by long gaps, and Enea has tracked it since early 2025. The firm says the operation has been disrupted for now but could return.

Who's affected

Mobile operators in Asia and their customers, especially government officials and other high-value people in Taiwan who rely on SMS codes.

What to do

Operators should treat inter-carrier signaling traffic as untrusted and deploy signaling firewalls. High-risk users should switch from SMS codes to authenticator apps or security keys.
